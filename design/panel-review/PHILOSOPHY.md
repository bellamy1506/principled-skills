# The philosophy of the panel

For whoever changes this skill. It is not loaded when the skill runs and
SKILL.md does not need it to work. Read it before you edit SKILL.md, so a
change made in a fresh context does not pull the skill off its purpose.

This is a distillate of the session that wrote the skill, of two months of
use before it, and of the first field runs after it. The reason behind
each rule is in the skill's `references/origins.md`.

## The one idea

A panel exists to produce thinking that one capable generalist would not
produce. Everything else in the skill serves that.

A model asked a question gives the answer its training makes most likely.
Ask three times and you get that answer three times, in different words.
This is the rut. A rut is not an error; the common answer is often good. But
it bounds which solutions are ever considered, and no amount of care inside
the rut finds what lies outside it.

A lens is a forced premise: one thing the reviewer must hold above all
others, pressed hard enough to beat the pull toward the common answer. It
makes the reviewer enter the problem from a side it would not choose. A
reviewer under a lens is worse as a generalist and better as a source.

The orchestrator kept its general judgement. So the orchestrator does the
one thing the lenses cannot: it combines. It takes the strongest element
from each report, cuts back what is unreasonable, raises what was revealed,
and builds a result that no single report contains.

## Two uses, one instrument

A panel reviews work its author cannot see clearly. It is equally the place
where a different way of doing something is first imagined. When small
steps have stopped paying, a panel looks for a new base to step from. The
companion skill, `council-testing`, is where such an idea is then built and
measured: a panel thinks differently, a council does differently. A panel
may feed a council only through its synthesis; one lens's report carries
its bend with it.

## What follows from it

- **The product is a synthesis.** Not a vote, not the overlap, not a middle
  position. If the output is something all three would have signed, the
  panel returned the average, and the average was free.
- **Distance between lenses is the fuel.** Three similar reports cannot be
  combined into something better than one of them. The round is won or lost
  when the lenses are chosen.
- **A lens that is "wrong" can still pay.** A reviewer pushed down a poor
  road may not arrive anywhere good. It may also pick up something on that
  road that nobody on the good road would see. The reviewer must not know
  its lens is the doubtful one, or it stops pushing.
- **Transfer is the largest prize.** One lens's failed idea can solve a
  different lens's problem. Only the orchestrator can see this, because
  only it reads all three.
- **A panelist does not build.** It traded balance for reach. Give it the
  build and you get a lopsided product.
- **A premise serves the goal.** "Cheapest" taken alone says to stop
  working. A lens is an extreme way toward what the owner wants, inside
  what is not negotiable.
- **The orchestrator's own view is the most dangerous leak.** A brief that
  carries a hypothesis moves all three lenses back into the orchestrator's
  rut. Evidence goes in; conclusions stay out. The view travels in
  everything the orchestrator writes: the goal line, the lens text, the
  questions, and the choice of which facts to count. The same holds in a
  second round: reviewers see each other's reports, never the synthesis.
- **A shared instrument is a shared rut.** Three lenses that look through
  one faulty stand-in return its fault three times, and it reads as
  agreement. The instrument is compared with the real thing before the
  round, not after.
- **The owner is the anchor at lens choice.** The round is won or lost
  there, and the orchestrator judges alone. So the dispatch message shows
  the owner the goal line and each lens with its expected headline.
- **Facts are verified; ideas are not put on trial for their lens's
  errors.** A reviewer is a clever junior: sometimes exactly right,
  sometimes a good idea badly built, sometimes confidently wrong. Check the
  facts the synthesis stands on. Do not throw away an idea because the same
  report got a count wrong.
- **A second round is for provocation.** A proved discovery can set off new
  ideas in the lenses. It can come from a lens, from the orchestrator's
  verification or from the owner: a measured fact is evidence whoever
  measured it, and only the orchestrator's reading of it is withheld. It
  is never a round for agreement.
  A seat with nothing new to receive gets a new lens or an adversarial task.
- **The orchestrator is the weakest point.** A council has a stopwatch to
  judge it. A panel has only the orchestrator, who sits in the very rut the
  lenses were built to leave. Trimming "the unreasonable" is where the odd,
  valuable idea is most easily lost. So the orchestrator must say what
  would have to be true for a rejected idea to work, and must name the
  strangest idea it kept and the strangest it dropped.
- **The design is hidden from the reviewers.** A reviewer that learns its
  lens may be the doubtful one, or that its report is raw material to be
  cut down, stops pushing. The skill's public description is neutral for
  this reason, the brief names which skills a reviewer may load, and a
  reviewer stays inside the project. README.md and this file say the
  design openly; they are for people and maintainers, and a brief never
  points at them.
- **Odd numbers.** A coarse disagreement on an assumption needs a tiebreak.
- **It is a deliberate, front-loaded cost.** It belongs to design, hard
  problems and trust decisions. The owner starts it. The models stay cheap
  because the value comes from difference, not from power.

## Tight here, loose there

**Keep tight** (these protect independence and honesty):

- evidence in a brief, never the orchestrator's conclusion or method;
- a goal line that states an outcome and its limits and nothing else;
- read-only reviewers; all dispatched together;
- reviewers never see the synthesis;
- facts are verified before the synthesis stands on them;
- lenses far apart, checked by their predicted headlines;
- the cheap model; the owner starts the round.

**Keep loose** (these are where the thinking happens):

- where a reviewer reads, once it has started from what was named;
- speculation, when it is labelled as an idea;
- how an idea is scored: payoff and odds apart, so a long shot survives;
- the report: fixed headings for comparison, a free section and room for
  what fits none of them. Length is not weight;
- which lenses, and how many of the old ones are reused: fit the subject.

A reviewer may say it found no defects. It may not decline to say what it
would do.

## The analogy to keep

A miner wants a better pickaxe. Put a chemist and an engineer beside him.
The chemist thinks of explosives and of finer extraction; the engineer
thinks of a drill. None of them alone should plan the mine. The chemist's
extraction compound may fail at extraction and sit unused, until the miner
throws it on bedrock that every plan had to go around, and the rock gives
way to an ordinary pick. The compound was not made for that. Now the whole
mine can be planned differently. Such a result is not likely on any one
round. The panel is built so that it is possible.

## How this skill has gone wrong before

- **It was read as a consensus instrument.** The oldest text said the three
  "must come to a consensus", and the first draft of this skill was built on
  that: reconcile, find what all three concede, report the agreement. The
  owner corrected it. If you find the word consensus coming back as a goal,
  stop.
- **Rule documents were read as intent.** The project the panel came from
  wrote rules for its own hooks and costs. Those are one implementation.
  When a record and this file disagree about purpose, this file is the
  purpose.
- **A panel with rounds was called a council.** A read-only reviewer that
  sees other reports is still a panelist.
- **Overlap was reported as independent agreement.** The orchestrator had
  written one claim into two lens paragraphs and the answer into a shared
  question.
- **A stand-in was trusted until someone looked at the real thing.** Three
  field panels in a row.
- **A project's record form replaced the report.** The sections that make
  the orchestrator show its work were dropped at filing.
- **A first review of field runs scored them against the steps.** The
  owner sent it back to this file.

## Tests for a proposed change

1. Does it make the three reports more alike? Then it is wrong.
2. Does it put the orchestrator's opinion in front of a reviewer? Wrong.
3. Does it ask reviewers to agree, concede, or converge? Wrong.
4. Does it let a panelist edit, build or patch? That is a council.
5. Does it let the round start, grow or change model without the owner?
   Only the cheap, proved cross-pollination round may.
6. Could a reviewer learn from it how its report will be used, or that its
   lens is in doubt? Wrong.
7. Does it make it easier for the orchestrator to drop an odd idea without
   a stated reason? Wrong.
8. Does it narrow what a reviewer may think, read or propose? Probably
   wrong.
9. Does it add a rule with no incident behind it and no check? Leave it out.
